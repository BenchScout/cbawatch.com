The deal lapses at 11:59 pm ET on December 1, 2026, and a lockout is expected the moment it does. This site argues that's an opportunity — for a salary cap, a salary floor, and a competitive integrity tax.

Three claims, held without apology.

1

MLB needs a salary cap.

The NFL, NBA, and NHL all operate under one. Baseball is the last major league where payroll is purely a function of an owner's ambition and a market's size — which means October is, more than anywhere else in sports, something you can buy.

2

The Dodgers are ruining the integrity of the game.

Not by cheating — by outspending. Back-to-back titles, a near-$400M tax payroll, and a deferral structure that pushes a billion dollars off today's books. A brilliant organization exploiting a broken system, in plain sight.

3

Teams that refuse to spend should pay for it.

A cap without a floor just enriches owners. Pair it with a competitive integrity tax: clubs that pocket revenue-sharing checks while fielding $75M rosters pay the shortfall — plus a penalty — to the teams that actually compete.

Buying today with tomorrow's money.

Shohei Ohtani's contract pays him $2M a year now and $68M a year from 2034. Repeat that structure across a roster and you can field a superteam whose true cost barely registers against the tax. It's not cheating. That's the problem — it's legal.

The villain here isn't Ohtani, and it isn't really the Dodgers' front office, which simply plays the written rules better than anyone. The villain is a CBA that lets present-day dominance be financed with accounting. A cap that counts deferred money at full value — in the season the player actually plays — closes the loophole in a sentence.

Three mechanisms. One principle:
pay to not compete.

Hard salary cap

A real ceiling, not a tax line the Dodgers treat as a cover charge. Deferred salary counts at full present value in the year it's earned. No one buys a five-year head start again.

Salary floor

If there's a ceiling in Los Angeles, there's a floor in Pittsburgh, Miami, and Sacramento. Every club commits a guaranteed minimum to major-league payroll — revenue sharing becomes roster, not profit.

Competitive integrity tax

Miss the floor, pay the difference plus a penalty into a pool for the clubs that do spend. Tanking stops being a business model the moment it comes with an invoice.

The union has always called a cap a non-starter — that fight is why the coming lockout could be long. But a cap with a floor is a different deal than the one the players struck down in 1994: it forces the bottom half of the league into the market, widens the bidding for every free agent, and gives a kid in Pittsburgh the same October odds as a kid in Los Angeles. That's worth missing spring training over.

How we got to the brink.

Dec 2021

The last lockout

Owners lock the players out for 99 days — the first stoppage in a quarter century. It ends with a higher tax threshold, no cap, and the core dispute postponed.

Dec 2023

The $680M deferral

Ohtani signs for ten years, $700 million — $680 million of it deferred to 2034–2043. Proof the tax system can be gamed at scale.

2024–25

Back-to-back

The Dodgers win consecutive World Series with the largest payroll in the sport's history. Most of the league starts its winter in November, knowing how the movie ends.

2025–26

Cap talk goes public

Ownership's appetite for a cap moves from whispers to open signaling. The union calls it a non-starter. Both sides prepare for a fight.

Dec 1, 2026

The CBA expires

11:59 pm ET. A lockout is expected the moment the clock strikes — the one point of leverage where the system can actually be rebuilt.

2027

The season at stake

Spring training, maybe more, rides on the outcome. If baseball returns with a cap, a floor, and an integrity tax — worth it.
